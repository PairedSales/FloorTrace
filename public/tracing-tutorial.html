FloorTrace · exterior boundary detection

Floorplan
to Polygon

A floorplan is a picture of a building. Square footage needs a polygon.

This page follows one 600 × 370 scan through the tracer that turns the first into the second. Every figure is real output from the pipeline in src/utils/detection/ — not a redrawing of it. The argument is that almost every step exists not to find the outline, but to stop a wrong outline from looking right.

00

A picture is not a shape

What exactly are we being asked for?

The input is a raster: a grid of colours with no notion of a wall, a room or a building. The output has to be a closed polygon in image coordinates, because everything downstream — area, the interior envelope, the exclusion of a garage — is polygon arithmetic. Between those two lies the whole problem.

Three answers look obvious and all three are wrong in instructive ways.

“Take the bounding box of the ink.” Buildings have notches. On this plan the true outline is 147,888 px² and its bounding box is 161,280 px² — 9.1% larger, or 46 ft² of a 512 ft² floor. A bounding box also passes every sanity check you could write for it, which is the problem.

“Flood-fill the inside.” From where? And the drawing leaks: every doorway is a literal gap in the wall, so a flood from inside runs out through the front door and covers the page.

“Find the largest closed contour.” There isn’t one. The exterior wall of a real plan is interrupted by every window, every slider, every garage bay. The outline you are looking for is not drawn.

The move the whole pipeline turns on

You cannot compute the outline from the pixels. You can only propose outlines and measure which one the drawing supports.

That reframing is what makes the rest tractable. Instead of one clever heuristic that must be right, the tracer runs four stages: reduce the picture to graded evidence; enumerate every reading of that evidence a careful person might defend; score each reading against the evidence rather than against a threshold; and hand over the winner with the reasons it might be wrong still attached to it.

The rest of this page is those four stages on one drawing.

01

Which pixels are drawn at all?

From 24-bit colour to one bit per pixel.

Every later stage is a question about sets of pixels — does this set enclose that set, how thick is this run, what fraction of this edge has ink behind it. Those questions are cheap on a binary mask and expensive on colour, so the first thing that happens is a reduction to one bit per pixel: ink[y * width + x] ∈ {0, 1}.

Colour collapses to luminance with an integer BT.601 luma, (r·77 + g·150 + b·29) >> 8, and the 256-bin histogram is accumulated in the same pass — a floorplan photographed at 50 MP would otherwise pay for a second full walk just to count.

Then the threshold. A scan has no canonical “black”: paper is not 255, ink is not 0, and both drift with the scanner. Otsu’s method answers this without a magic number. Imagine sliding a threshold t and splitting every pixel into two piles. Score the split by how far apart the two piles’ means are, weighted by how big the piles are — that product is the between-class variance. The threshold that maximises it is the one where the two piles are most convincingly two things rather than one thing cut in half.

σ²between(t) = ω0(t)·ω1(t)·(μ0(t) − μ1(t))²   →   t* = 134 on this plan

Two guards on a textbook algorithm

Plain Otsu fails twice in this domain, so inkThreshold wraps it. On a colour-styled plan the split lands between the tinted room fills and the page white, turning whole floors into one solid slab with the walls, cavities and text swallowed inside it. So when the dark class comes back implausibly large for line work — over 14% of the page — the dark class is split again, and the lower threshold is kept only if the two dark modes are genuinely far apart, which distinguishes strokes-plus-fills from grey hatching. This drawing sits at 11.9% dark, just under the trigger, so plain Otsu stands.

And the result is clamped to [60, 220]. For a perfectly bi-level input, between-class variance is maximised at the bottom of the range, so plain Otsu returns 0 and classifies nothing as ink. The clamp is not a fudge factor; it is the fix for a degenerate case the formula genuinely has.

The downscale that isn’t here

Above 1,400 px on the long edge the raster is reduced — and it is reduced after thresholding, by OR-pooling: a working pixel is ink if any source pixel in its cell was. Averaging or nearest-neighbour sampling both delete one-pixel hairlines, and a hairline is often the only thing drawn along a bay window. The greyscale is box-averaged alongside in the same pass, because the tint tests further down need tone, not just presence. This plan is 600 px wide, so none of it runs: scaleX = 1.0, and every coordinate on this page is a real image coordinate.

02

Which ink is a wall?

Most of what is drawn is not structure.

The ink mask contains the walls and also: room names, dimension strings, a toilet, a sink, a stair and its treads, every door swing, cabinet outlines and a dashed soffit line. All of it is equally black. Three filters separate structure from the rest, and each asks a different question.

a. Size — connected components

A 4-connected labelling pass (a scanline flood that pushes whole spans onto its stack rather than pixels) finds every maximal blob of touching ink. This drawing has 372 of them. 366 fit inside an 18 px box and are erased: 98% of the components, but only 6.2% of the ink. What survives is six components, the largest of which — 24,182 px — is the wall network itself.

The 18 comes from max(14, 3% of the long edge). Every threshold in this pipeline is expressed relative to the drawing rather than to the paper, because “small” means small compared to the building, not small in absolute pixels.

b. Shape — long straight runs

A drafted line has a property nothing else in the drawing has: it runs straight for a long way. So the mask is scanned for runs of at least 12 px in four directions — horizontal, vertical, and both diagonals — on a copy dilated by one pixel, so a scan line that wobbles by a pixel over 40 px still reads as one run rather than four short ones. Union that with everything surviving a 5 × 5 morphological opening, which catches thick walls of any shape, and you have wallMask.

c. Thickness — how thick is a wall here?

Nothing downstream can be written in absolute pixels, because the same building might arrive at 600 px or 4,000. So the pipeline measures one number and derives the rest from it. Every ink run in every row and column is histogrammed by length, each weighted by its own length so that pixel mass decides rather than run count, and the mode is taken.

Three masks, three readers

The stage does not produce one answer to “is this a wall”, because two consumers need different answers and pretending otherwise breaks one of them.

strict33,805

wallMask — long runs plus thick blobs. Room detection uses this one. If a door arc read as wall here, a room rectangle would stop growing at the door instead of at the wall behind it.

structural27,020

thickMask — wallMask opened at 3 px (0.3 × wall thickness). Strokes thick enough to be load-bearing. This is what “full evidence” means when a candidate outline is scored.

generous35,959

boundaryMask — wallMask plus rescued line-like residual ink plus screened glazing. The exterior tracer uses this one, because a bay window drawn in hairlines is still the outside of the building even though no room rectangle should ever stop against it.

The rescue that pays for itself: screened glazing

Some plans draw a window as two black rails with paper showing between them. This one draws it as a grey band filling the wall. Above the ink threshold that band is not there at all — so as far as every filter above is concerned, the exterior wall has a hole in it exactly the width of the window.

You cannot fix this by relaxing the threshold, because the same grey is used for stair treads and floor tint. And you cannot fix it by widening the gap-welding, because a window that runs to within a wall-thickness of a corner leaves a stub too short to weld against — and the minimum stub length cannot be relaxed either, since one wall-thickness is also what a scan line sees crossing a diagonal wall.

So the band is rescued directly, under five conditions that together describe “this grey band is the wall”: it is screened (at least 12 levels below the page’s own modal tone); it is at least half a wall thick and three walls long; it is no thicker than 1.8 × the wall; it fills at least 55% of its own bounding box; and — the test that does the real work — at both ends it continues into a wall whose cross-section is the band’s own.

That last condition is what separates a window from everything else drawn in the same grey. A stair tread runs between two walls, so the wall it meets crosses it rather than lining up with it. On this plan the rescue finds five bands, 2,154 px.

The failure mode everything after this is built against

Not a crash, not an obviously broken outline — a wrong answer that looks right. 24.5% of the building, 125 ft², missing, at 96% confidence, silently. Every remaining stage of the pipeline should be read as an answer to the question: how would we have caught this?

03

What does this ink enclose?

Turning strokes into regions — and refusing to pick just one.

Before anything can be enclosed, the page has to be divided into buildings. A single sheet often carries four plans; a plan often carries a legend, a north arrow and a title block. So the wall mask is dilated by twice the wall thickness to associate nearby strokes, the result is labelled, and the original wall pixels are projected back onto those groups. Each group is a wall network.

Groups then merge, but under a rule with two halves that a plain bounding-box overlap cannot express:

  • Extents must interleave, not contain. A floor outline broken into pieces by a long window has pieces that each cover part of the same region — their boxes overlap partially. A legend drawn inside an L-plan’s notch is contained. Those are opposite cases and look identical to “do the boxes overlap”.
  • Neither piece may seal on its own. If two groups each close into their own footprint, they are two drawings — however neatly their walls happen to line up on the page. This is what stops two floor plans stacked on a sheet, sharing a left-wall coordinate, from being welded into one impossible building.

This plan is a single network: 35,959 wall pixels, spanning [20, 25] to [580, 360].

Closing, flooding, labelling

Given one network, turning it into a region is three primitives. Morphological closing at radius r — dilate by r, then erode by r — welds shut any gap narrower than 2r while leaving everything wider than that alone; it is what makes a doorway stop being a hole. Then flood from the page border: every pixel the outside can reach is outside. Invert, and what remains is enclosed. Label the result and take the largest component, and you have a footprint.

Which leaves exactly one question: what is r?

The second move the pipeline turns on

Don’t pick r. Climb it, and keep every rung as a competing hypothesis.

The ladder is geometric — r ← round(1.45·r) + 1 from r = 2, capped at max(32, 4.5% of the long edge) — giving 2, 4, 7, 11, 17, 26, 32 here. Every rung whose enclosed area actually changed becomes a candidate. Two evidence variants are climbed independently: all, everything in the boundary mask, and structural, only strokes surviving a 3 px opening. And a connectivity policy sits under each: weld welds collinear runs across gaps but refuses to weld across a notch mouth; raw welds nothing, kept as a control on whether the welding invented the answer; span paints wall lines across their full extent, a rescue that runs only when nothing enclosed at all.

Sealed is not the same as complete

Switch the widget above to structural / weld and hold it at r = 2. That footprint seals perfectly. Its cover — how much of the wall network’s bounding box it spans — is 0.86; its solidity — how much of its own box it fills — is 0.56; the combined seal score is 1.00. Its outline is drawn as wall along 95.2% of its length, better than the candidate that eventually wins. It passes every test that can be applied to it in isolation.

And it is missing an entire bedroom.

Nothing local can see this. The only thing that can is the ladder itself: the same evidence, climbed to r = 26, encloses 149,071 px instead of 90,305. That ratio is completeness = 0.606, and it is the sole reason the pipeline does not return a plan with a room cut off it.

Choosing the ladder’s reference

“The most the same evidence eventually enclosed” cannot just be the largest rung, because a large enough radius eventually swallows the neighbouring plan. So the reference is taken from a truncated ladder: cut at the first rung that fuses two separate enclosures into one — that rung joined two drawings — then drop a trailing tail still growing faster than 3% per rung — that is annexation, not sealing — and take the maximum of what is left. A sealed footprint gains only rounding slack from extra radius; one still growing at the top of the ladder is eating the page.

04

Which reading is the building?

Five candidates, four measurements, three contradictions.

The search produced five distinct footprints for this network. Each is scored on four things the drawing can be asked about, combined as a weighted mean — and then three quantities are subtracted from outside that mean, which turns out to be the most consequential design decision in the file.

weight0.34

seal — did it close, and does it fill its own network? √(coverScore × solidScore), where cover is the footprint’s box against the wall network’s box and solidity is the footprint against its own box. A geometric mean, so a half-sealed wing reads as “not sealed yet” rather than “close enough on average”.

weight0.30

support — is this outline actually drawn as a wall? Walk the polygon in 2 px steps and look up the evidence level within 10 px (⌈0.9 × wall thickness⌉). Graded, never binary: structural ink = 1.0, any wall stroke = 0.45, raw ink = 0.2, nothing = 0. Length-weighted mean. The grading is what lets a fabricated edge across blank paper be told apart from a real bay drawn in hairlines.

It also records the longest unsupported run. The mean says how much of the outline is drawn; the run says whether the missing part is one invented wall or noise spread thin.

weight0.26

coverage — of the wall ink in this network, weighted by how structural it is (thick 1.0, thin 0.25), what fraction ends up inside? Leaving drawn wall outside your outline does not mean you were conservative; it means you lost part of the building.

weight0.10

economy — 1 − (0.6·r/rmax + 0.4·spanCost). Closing radius and welded span are both fabrication. Between two readings that fit the drawing equally well, prefer the one that invented less.

subtract0.30

annex — the footprint’s box reaches beyond the wall network it grew from. It has taken something that is not this floor.

subtract0.30

incomplete — (0.97 − completeness)/0.2, clamped. The bedroom-losing candidate, caught.

subtract0.30

excluded — a room or a parsed dimension label that the rest of the app already established is inside the building, and this outline puts outside.

Why three terms sit outside the mean

Inside a weighted mean, a contradiction is just a low mark, and good behaviour elsewhere buys it back. That is not a theoretical worry — it was measured. As a reward term, holding all the known-inside labels was worth 22/122 of the score, times one label in nine. On a plan whose bottom-right corner is drawn open, the only candidate that enclosed every label lost by 0.005 to one that left an entire storage room outside — because the winner had a tidier closing radius and bought the miss back on economy alone.

Leaving a room outside is not a slightly worse outline. It is a contradiction with something already known, and it belongs in the same place as annex and incomplete: subtracted, after the averaging, where nothing can pay for it.

Ties break toward the cheaper hypothesis

Here all/weld @ r2 and all/raw @ r2 score identically: 0.9782, same seal, same support, same area. The tie-break order is less invented evidence, then less closing, then less bridging — so raw, which welded nothing at all, wins. The welding was never needed on this drawing, and the pipeline says so by preferring the hypothesis that didn’t use it.

One subtlety worth stealing: “within ε of each other” is not transitive, so it cannot be a sort comparator. Three candidates with a≈b, b≈c, a<c made the winner a function of the order they happened to be pushed in — reversing the input array changed the returned footprint. Candidates are now compared against the leader, and only then broken by cost.

Confidence is not the score

The score ranks; the confidence reports, and it is computed separately and pessimistically. It starts at seal × (0.3 + 0.7 × support) and is then multiplied down by every defect found: unsealed ×0.35, weak support ×0.75, a bridged opening scaled by its length, a heavy closing ×0.85, annexation, incompleteness ×0.7, wall left outside, a missing constraint. It is clamped to [0.05, 0.98] — this tracer is never allowed to say it is certain.

confidence = 1.000 × (0.3 + 0.7 × 0.940) = 0.958  — no penalties applied on this plan
05

From a region to fourteen vertices

The winner is still a bitmap. Area needs geometry.

Moore boundary tracing walks the outside of the component: from a start pixel, scan the 8-neighbourhood clockwise beginning just past the direction you came from, step to the first neighbour that belongs to the region, and repeat. The subtle part is knowing when to stop. Stopping when you re-enter the start pixel is wrong — a one-pixel isthmus makes the walk pass through the start mid-loop, and the ring is silently truncated with part of the outline missing. Jacob’s criterion stops on the repeated state: same pixel and same incoming direction. That returns 1,800 boundary pixels here.

Ramer–Douglas–Peucker

1,800 points is a staircase, not an outline. RDP simplifies it recursively: take the chord from the first point to the last, find the point farthest from that chord, and if it is farther than ε, keep it and recurse on both halves; otherwise discard everything between. What survives is exactly the points that carry shape.

ε is max(2, 0.35 × wallThickness) = 3.85 px — scale-relative again, so a 4,000 px scan of the same building simplifies to the same corners. One detail matters more than it looks: the ring is first rotated to start at the point farthest from its centroid, which is almost certainly a corner. RDP’s first chord is its entire frame of reference, and anchoring it mid-wall makes every later split worse.

The rectilinear fit

RDP puts corners in roughly the right places but leaves edges at 89.4°, because it is choosing from pixels on a staircase. Buildings are drawn on axes, so the ring is refit: classify every edge as horizontal, vertical or genuinely diagonal within a 14° tolerance; refit each as a line, weighted by length; merge consecutive same-orientation lines sitting within 6 px (half a wall thickness) of each other, which removes RDP’s jitter along a single wall face; insert a perpendicular jog between consecutive parallel lines, which restores a real step whose short connector RDP dropped; then intersect consecutive lines to produce the vertices. 17 points become 14.

Intersecting rather than snapping is the point. Snapping moves each vertex independently and opens the ring at the corners; intersecting two fitted lines yields one point that both edges agree on by construction.

Before any of that, the ring’s dominant orientation is measured — a length-weighted histogram of edge angles at 0.5° resolution, folded into 90° and smoothed so a chamfer cannot out-vote the walls — and if it lands between 0.75° and 20°, the ring is rotated flat, fitted, and rotated back. Scans and phone photos routinely sit several degrees off axis, and fitting such a ring straight onto the image axes flattens the shape while leaving the area almost unchanged, which means no area check and no bounding-box check can ever catch it. This plan measures 0.25°, so the de-skew does not fire — but the measured angle is reported either way, because past 20° the ring really is squashed onto the page’s axes and that is precisely the case worth telling someone about.

Area, and the sign that matters

Area is the signed shoelace sum, with holes subtracted. Signed, deliberately, and the absolute value is taken as late as possible: fold it in early and a self-intersecting polygon’s lobes cancel, so the area quietly under-reports instead of the self-intersection being detected.

06

How does it know when it’s wrong?

Nothing above proves the answer is right.

Everything so far produces a polygon. None of it produces a guarantee. So the last thing the tracer does is carry its own doubts forward: alongside the geometry it returns warnings[], each with a code, a severity, a sentence in plain English, and an anchor — the place on the image to look. A warning nobody can point at is unactionable, and “a 34 px opening was bridged” is exactly that until you can see which opening.

Twenty-five codes carry a written explanation, and three more are raised by the non-GLA stage. unsealed, weak-wall-support, bridged-opening, heavy-closing, annexation, incomplete-enclosure, wall-left-outside, spanned-walls, self-intersecting, label-outside, enclosed-void. This plan raises none of them, which is why its confidence stays at the value the formula produced.

Searching again, on evidence the search cannot see

If the winner comes back below 0.75 effective confidence, or excludes a room the app already knows is inside, the whole search runs a second time under two strategies. join re-runs the wall networks surrounding a stranded room as a single network — the case where one drawing was partitioned into pieces, which no closing radius can ever reach, because closing operates within a network and the mistake was the partition. escalate forces every rescue policy and doubles the ladder over the same partition.

Two details are load-bearing. Adjudication is on effective confidence — the detector’s own number multiplied by a constraint factor — because constraints are scoped per network, so inside the network that caused the miss the miss is invisible: the base attempt can honestly report 0.93 while the app is showing the user 0.47. And escalate is gated on the ladder’s ceiling actually having been reached. The ladder is climbed from r = 2 with every rung scored, so a winner that sealed well below the top has already beaten every wider rung that exists; extending the ladder only appends rungs that score worse on economy. Ungated, that pass doubled the trace time of every merely-fair plan and was never once accepted.

Evidence flowing backwards

The OCR pass that reads 12'2" × 12'5" off this drawing is not only producing a scale. Every parsed label is a point that is inside the building by construction, and every confirmed room is a rectangle that is inside it. Those arrive back at the tracer as constraints: geometry that excludes one is scored down, and a winner that still excludes one is re-searched. A pipeline stage that runs later feeding a stage that runs earlier is unusual, and it is the single most valuable input the tracer gets, because it is the only evidence that did not come from the same pixels it is judging.

And what is not living area

A traced footprint is the building; gross living area is less than that. Four detectors propose regions to remove — OCR keyword votes, label floods, geometric garage evidence, and shaded pockets — overlapping proposals merge into one region carrying both sources, and a single pass removes them under a cumulative bound, so the result cannot depend on which detector ran first.

The shaded-pocket detector is treated differently in three separate places, and the reason is a good closing note for the whole page: a tinted bathroom floor and a condo balcony are the same pixels. Only a label can tell them apart. So a pocket that a parsed dimension is written on is refused outright; the carve does not join the region list that exempts labels from the “label outside the outline” check, so it cannot silence the very evidence that contradicts it; and its warning is severity warn rather than info, so it is counted among the things a person should check. Ungoverned, those three together once deleted two bathrooms from a house at 93% confidence with nothing said — the same failure as the ablation at the top of this page, arriving by a completely different route.

The shape of it

Generate, measure, explain

Read as a list of steps, this pipeline is a dozen morphological operations in a row. Read as an argument, it is one idea applied four times: the picture cannot be asked what the building is, so ask it many questions whose answers can be compared.

Reduce the image to graded evidence rather than to a decision. Enumerate every reading of that evidence a careful person might defend, including the ones you expect to lose. Score them against the evidence instead of thresholding any single one. Then hand over the winner with its confidence and its doubts still attached, because the alternative — a clean number with no provenance — is the one outcome that cannot be caught downstream.

That shape is why the tracer can be right about a drawing nobody anticipated. It is also why, on the drawing where it is wrong, it can usually say so.
